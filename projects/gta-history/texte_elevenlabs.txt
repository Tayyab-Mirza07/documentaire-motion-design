The mid nineteen-nineties. In Dundee, Scotland, a small studio is building a game about cops chasing robbers through a city. The testers are bored. The project is in trouble, and close to being cancelled.
<break time="2.0s" />
Then a bug appears. The police cars turn aggressive. They stop chasing the player's car and start ramming it off the road. It is pure chaos. And suddenly, the testers cannot stop playing.
<break time="2.0s" />
That accident became Grand Theft Auto. Nearly four hundred and eighty million copies sold. Debates in parliament. Court cases. Some of the biggest launches entertainment has ever seen. This is how a glitch became a giant.
<break time="2.0s" />
The studio was called DMA Design. It was founded by David Jones, and it was already famous for the puzzle game Lemmings. Its new project had a working title: Race'n'Chase. A living city, seen from above.
<break time="2.0s" />
Players could be the cop or the criminal. Almost nobody chose the cop. Obeying the rules was slow. Breaking them was fun. So the team followed the fun, and gave the game a new name: Grand Theft Auto.
<break time="2.0s" />
It was released in late 1997, on PC and then PlayStation. Tiny cars, a bird's-eye view, and three cities to cause trouble in: Liberty City, Vice City and San Andreas. Remember those names. They come back.
<break time="2.0s" />
Before the release, the publisher hired a tabloid publicist, Max Clifford. His plan was to stir up outrage, on purpose. It worked. In May 1997, months before anyone could buy it, the game was raised in the House of Lords.
<break time="2.0s" />
Every angry headline was free advertising. British censors gave the game an eighteen certificate, and it sold anyway. The series had found its formula: total freedom, dark satire, and controversy as fuel.
<break time="2.0s" />
Two British brothers were watching closely: Sam and Dan Houser. In 1998, in New York, they helped found a new label inside the publisher Take-Two. They called it Rockstar Games. A sequel followed in 1999. The real leap came next.
<break time="2.0s" />
October 2001. Grand Theft Auto III brings the camera down to street level, on PlayStation 2. For the first time, Liberty City is a full three-dimensional world. You can walk it, drive it, and get lost in it.
<break time="2.0s" />
The timing was brutal. The game was set in a version of New York, and it was due weeks after September 11. Rockstar delayed it, cut a few pieces of content, and redesigned the cover. The changes were small: about one percent of the game, by Rockstar's own estimate.
<break time="2.0s" />
The idea was simple. Here is a city. Do what you want. Follow the story, or ignore it. Steal a taxi and pick up fares. Rival studios took notes, and within a few years the open world was a genre of its own.
<break time="2.0s" />
One year later came Vice City. A neon postcard of nineteen-eighties Miami: pastel suits, palm trees, a radio full of real hits from the decade, and Hollywood actors in the cast. Grand Theft Auto was not only a game any more. It was pop culture.
<break time="2.0s" />
In 2004, San Andreas went bigger again. Three cities, countryside and desert on one single map, set in early-nineties California. You could swim, fly a plane, get a haircut, even put on weight. It became the best-selling game on the PlayStation 2.
<break time="2.0s" />
Then, in 2005, modders found something left on the disc. An unfinished sex mini-game, nicknamed Hot Coffee. The developers had switched it off. They had never actually removed it.
<break time="2.0s" />
America's ratings board changed the game's rating to Adults Only. Big retailers pulled it from their shelves. Take-Two reported more than twenty-four million dollars in costs from returns, and settled with the Federal Trade Commission a year later.
<break time="2.0s" />
By now, the series stood at the centre of a much larger argument. Do violent games harm the people who play them? Campaigners and politicians called for bans. Defenders replied that these were satires, rated for adults.
<break time="2.0s" />
April 2008. Grand Theft Auto IV returns to Liberty City, with a darker story. Its hero is Niko Bellic, an immigrant from Eastern Europe, chasing an American dream that keeps slipping out of reach.
<break time="2.0s" />
On its first day, it sold three point six million copies and took in three hundred and ten million dollars. After one week: five hundred million. Guinness World Records certified it as the highest-grossing video game in twenty-four hours.
<break time="2.0s" />
Then, in September 2013, Grand Theft Auto V. Three playable criminals, Michael, Franklin and Trevor, sharing one enormous Los Santos. It made eight hundred million dollars in twenty-four hours, and passed one billion within three days.
<break time="2.0s" />
The real surprise came weeks later, at no extra cost. Grand Theft Auto Online turned that city into a shared playground, and kept adding to it, year after year. The players stayed. So did the sales.
<break time="2.0s" />
The same game was sold across three generations of consoles. First PlayStation 3 and Xbox 360, then the next generation, then the one after that. By September 2026, it had reached around two hundred and thirty-five million copies. Thirteen years old, and still on the charts.
<break time="2.0s" />
For the fans, that success had a price: the wait. Year after year, there was no new chapter. Then, in 2022, a hacker leaked dozens of clips from the next game, still in early development.
<break time="2.0s" />
December 2023. The first trailer for Grand Theft Auto VI finally arrives, ten years after the last game. It passes ninety million views on YouTube in a single day. A Guinness World Record for a video game reveal.
<break time="2.0s" />
The second trailer, in May 2025, went even further. Rockstar counted four hundred and seventy-five million views across all platforms in twenty-four hours. The release date, meanwhile, slipped twice: from 2025 to May 2026, and then to November.
<break time="2.0s" />
Now the date is set. November nineteenth, 2026, on PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series. A return to Vice City, in a state called Leonida, with two leads: Jason and Lucia. Thirteen years after the last numbered game.
<break time="2.0s" />
So what is Grand Theft Auto? For its critics, a bad influence. For its fans, a sharp parody of America: its money, its media, its excess. For the industry, the standard everyone else is measured against.
<break time="2.0s" />
And all of it leads back to Dundee. To a failing racing game, and to police cars that would not stop ramming the player. Most bugs get fixed. This one built an empire.
